# Summary spec

A summary is a retelling. Someone who has never opened the book should finish it knowing the whole story,
every beat, and the world it happens in, and should have enjoyed the read. It is not a study guide.

## Shape (fiction)

About 90% of the words belong to **The story**, which carries the world and the characters inside it.

1. **Title and standfirst.** One italic line: a hook for the book, the word count, "full spoilers".
2. **Content note**, only when the book has heavy material (abuse, suicide, graphic violence): one quoted
   line naming it. Describe such events plainly in the story: what happens and why it matters, not how.
3. **Coverage note**, only when the story bible reports thin or conflicting sources: what is well sourced and
   what isn't.
4. **In brief.** Two or three sentences: who wrote it and when, and the premise, without the ending.
5. **The story.** Everything that happens, in order, with the world and the people built into it. See the
   rules below. There is no separate world tour and no cast list (the user asked for this on 2026-11-23: the
   worldbuilding belongs "in the plot summary, not a sort of dictionary").
6. **What it all means.** One short section, a few paragraphs: the main ideas, how to read the ending, and
   the big disagreements, each tied to a scene the reader has just read. No lists of critics; name one only
   when the argument is known by their name. If the ending is unresolved, the story section says plainly
   what the text shows, and this section gives the main theories, labeled as theories.
7. **Sources.** One short italic line with the check date and counts (beats covered, claims confirmed,
   corrected, cut), then the source links.

## The story: every beat

- Cover every beat on the story bible's list, in order. A beat is any event that changes a character's
  situation, a relationship or the story's direction, plus the ending. Beats that live in notes, appendices or
  flashbacks count too.
- Follow the book's own divisions (parts, chapters), with markers so the reader can find their place. If the
  book tells its story out of order, either keep its order with clear dates, or retell it chronologically with
  a short note on how the book really arranges it; the tailoring note says which.
- Explain the ending fully: who ends up where, what it costs, what is left open.

## The world and the people, inside the story

Worldbuilding is a main event in fiction (the user asked for this on 2026-11-21, for every fiction summary),
and it lives in the narrative (2026-11-23). There is no tour before the story and no glossary.

- **Introduce each thing when it first matters.** A place when the plot enters it: its look, sound and feel in
  a few vivid sentences. A device, rule, institution or term when someone uses it or runs into it: what it is
  and exactly how it works, in a sentence or two, then back to the action. History when it explains what is
  happening now.
- **Let it grow.** When the plot returns to a place or leans harder on a rule, add the new layer then, not all
  up front. By the end the reader should know the world as well as from a separate tour, but have learned it
  the way the hero did.
- **Characters the same way.** Each person arrives with a line or two on who they are, what they want and how
  they come across, at their first entrance. Their arc is told by what they do.
- **Give it room.** In a book with an invented or heavily imagined world (science fiction, fantasy, dystopia,
  alternate history, a stylized society), the world-building passages can be generous, and that is wanted.
  In a realistic novel this is the setting, era and social rules, woven in lightly.
- **Tell each fact once**, at the moment it lands hardest.
- "What it all means" says what the world is saying.
- Length has no cap and no floor. It is set by the number of beats. Stop when every beat is told. Never pad,
  and never drop beats to save words. Report the real word count.

## An entertaining read

- **Tell scenes, don't list events.** Open each stretch with what someone wants or fears; let "so", "but"
  and "until" carry one event into the next; end stretches on the turn that pulls the reader forward.
- **Give the big scenes room.** The confrontation, the reveal, the death, the escape get a real paragraph
  with the confirmed details that make them vivid: an object, an image, a line of dialogue.
- **Match the book's voice.** Funny when the book is funny, tense when it's tense, strange when it's strange.
  A dry joke is welcome where the book earns it.
- **Prose, with evocative subheadings.** No bullet-point plots. Tables only for genuine reference (a
  calendar, a family tree, a list of factions).
- **Use short quotes that land**, only with wording confirmed in the story bible. Never invent a quote.
- **Cut what bores.** Publication trivia, prizes, sales, biography that doesn't change the reading, critic
  name-dropping, "this timeless classic", any sentence that could describe any book.
- The test: would a friend who hasn't read the book keep reading to find out what happens next?

## Visuals

Most summaries have no pictures. The user found them distracting in fiction (2026-11-23): "scrap the
visuals", "only have them for books like Gödel Escher Bach where the visuals elevate the meaning".

- **Fiction: no figures, no mood art, no photos.** The prose carries the world.
- **A figure only when it elevates the meaning:** it explains an idea better than prose can (a proof, a
  mechanism, a formal structure, a worked example drawn out), as in Gödel, Escher, Bach. Not decoration, not a
  recap of what the text already says, not a map or cast tree for a novel. Most nonfiction books need none
  either; a concept-heavy one may need several.
- **When there are figures:** original diagrams drawn into the doc, one look throughout (the same shapes and
  type sizes, one accent color for the thing that matters), a title that says what the figure shows, a short
  caption, only facts from the story bible.
- **Real images** only when public domain or freely licensed (Wikimedia Commons, a museum's open-access
  page), each with an italic caption giving its source and license. Show the user the list (file name, source,
  license, size) and get their go-ahead before downloading. They go in after all the text.
- **Pictures under copyright** (the book's own illustrations, modern artworks, film stills, covers): describe
  them, link to an official or museum page, and where the idea matters redraw the idea as an original
  diagram. Never copy them.
- **Look at every figure** as rendered, and fix anything that overflows, overlaps, crosses a label or is too
  small to read.

## Nonfiction

Same spirit. The argument, step by step, is the plot: every chapter's claim and the example or story that
carries it, in order, told as a line of thought that builds. The key concepts, terms and cases are the world:
introduce them up front and at first use. "What it all means" becomes the strongest objections and what the
book changed, still short.

## Honesty

- Write from the story bible only. A detail that isn't in it is looked up or left out.
- Where sources disagree, or something couldn't be confirmed, say so inline in a few words.
- The checker's standard: every beat covered, every factual claim matching the bible or a looked-up source.
  Wrong claims are fixed; unverifiable ones are hedged or cut.
